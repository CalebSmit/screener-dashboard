The screener, tested on its own history

Does the ranking predict returns?

Before trusting a ranking, ask whether it would have worked. This page tests the screener's weighting, and 17 alternatives from the investing literature, on fifteen years of S&P 500 history rebuilt from the financial statements companies had actually published at each date.

  • Not measurably. The ranking's average monthly rank correlation with the next month's return was −0.003 over 2012–2019 (95% interval −0.023 to +0.017) and 0.000 over 2020–2026 (95% interval −0.027 to +0.027). Pooled over all 176 months it was −0.002 (95% interval −0.018 to +0.015). Every interval includes zero. The pooled record could reliably detect an average of about 0.032 at a t-statistic of 3, and no smaller.
  • No alternative did better. None of the 17 alternative weightings cleared the bar set before the test, in either period.
  • The test can see a signal when there is one. Fed information it could not have had at the time (today's filings, for past months), the same test finds a strong one.

Reading the numbers. A rank correlation runs from −1 to +1: at +1 the ranking would have put every stock's next-month return in exact order, and at 0 it said nothing about them. A t-statistic is a result divided by its own standard error, so the further it is from 0, the less likely chance alone produced the result. A basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point.

How it was tested

Test enough ideas on one history and some look excellent by luck. The test was set up to prevent that:

  1. 1Every idea, its source and its predicted result were written down and published in the project's history before it was run. For seven of them (each category on its own, marked †), the 2020–2026 result had already been computed in an earlier check of the data before they were registered, so for those only the 2012–2019 test is blind.
  2. 2Ideas were compared with the current weighting month by month and judged on months they were never tuned on. Each comparison is paired by month and measured on the stocks the two rankings share, so a difference is a difference in ranking rather than in which companies were ranked. A weighting can only rank what it has enough data to score: over 2020–2026 the current weighting ranked 440 stocks a month on average and the narrowest alternative, Free-cash-flow yield alone (within sector), 348. The narrower screen ranks almost only companies the current weighting also ranks (fewer than one a month is unique to it), so pairing on the overlap holds the current weighting to that smaller set instead of crediting it with the 92 only it can score.
  3. 3The years 2012–2019 were set aside as an untouched test, with the rule for passing it, before any of that data was built.
  4. 4A result counts only if it clears a bar that allows for how many ideas were tried: p < 0.05 / 28 and a t-statistic of at least 3, the threshold Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) propose for a new factor. With p from Student's t, that means a t-statistic of at least 3.21 over 2012–2019's 96 months and 3.37 over the 38 held-out months. Every run is kept, including runs a data correction replaced.

Size alone (within sector)† shows the largest 2012–2019 difference, +0.027 (t +1.83), short of the pass bar. Survivorship could explain it. The test can only include companies that are still in the index today, and among 2012's members the small ones that survived are disproportionately the ones that did well. This data cannot separate that explanation from the passage of time. The share of each month's members the test could include rose steadily, from 57% in 2012 to 97% in 2026 (its correlation with the calendar is 0.998), and within 2012–2019 the Size screen's monthly result moved with that share about as much as the current weighting's did (correlations of −0.126 and −0.122). Rebuilding the departed members from free filings and prices would test it; that rebuild is queued with its prediction written down in advance.

What a simple trading rule on the ranking would have done

A rule that holds the top 25, keeps each until it falls past rank 40, skip names flagged as value or growth traps, at most 6 in a sector, 5 basis points of cost a side, rebalanced monthly. It is compared with the equal-weighted average return of the stocks the test could score each month. Those are the members still listed today: 57% of each month's members in 2012, rising to 97% in 2026, so the average leans toward the companies that survived.

The ranking's monthly correlation swings by about 0.12 from one month to the next, so any monthly ranking of large companies needs years of record before a realistic edge can be told apart from luck. That applies to this screener's live track record too. Here, the smallest difference the test could reliably see was about 0.038 over 2012–2019 and 0.062 over the held-out months.

What this means

Supported

Every factor the screener uses has a published source, and the Factor Lab reruns the studies behind its categories on public data. Every score on the dashboard can be rebuilt from its inputs. The testing is pre-registered and every run is kept.

Not supported

That the ranking beats the market, or that its top names will do better than the rest. On public data it has not been shown to predict returns, and the factor premiums it draws on were mostly weaker after their papers were published and weaker again among large companies.

So the screener is a structured, checkable way to look at a company across eight dimensions. It is not a forecast.

Limits of the test

  • It cannot include the analyst-based inputs (estimate revisions and forward earnings growth, about a sixth of the live weighting): no free history of them exists.
  • Companies that have since left the index are missing, which matters most in the earliest years.
  • Large companies only, rebalanced monthly, with costs approximated and no taxes.
