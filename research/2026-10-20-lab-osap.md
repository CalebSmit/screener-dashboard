# Factor Lab: Open Source Asset Pricing, and Piotroski (2000) at the portfolio level

**Date:** 2026-10-20. **Kind:** a data-source evaluation and a lab rerun.
**Not a proposal.** Nothing here changes a weight or a metric; the lab never justifies a screener
parameter by itself (`plan/factor-lab.md`, rule 1, and "From the lab to the weights").

## 1. Why

`plan/factor-lab.md`'s queue ends with a row the lab could not close: *Piotroski (2000), Beneish
(1999), analyst revisions (Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok 1996), post-earnings drift - cannot be
rerun on free data*, because each needs decades of company-level accounts or estimates. The owner
asked on 2026-10-20 what other free data would help, and the plan's "Free data to evaluate" table
named Chen & Zimmermann's Open Source Asset Pricing (OSAP) as the library that might close it: its
authors build portfolio returns for 200+ published predictors from CRSP and Compustat, and publish
the returns. A portfolio-level rerun needs only the returns.

The Piotroski score is the first choice because the screener weights it: 16% of Quality, so about
3.5 composite points (halved for companies whose valuation score is below the middle,
`config.yaml` `piotroski_conditional`), and the value-trap flag's "cheap" condition cites the
paper (2026-10-09).

## 2. The data: what it is, how to get it, what its terms allow

**What it is.** Andrew Y. Chen (Federal Reserve Board) and Tom Zimmermann (University of Cologne),
"Open source cross-sectional asset pricing", *Critical Finance Review* 11(2), 207-264 (2022). The
authors reimplement the signals of ~200 published cross-sectional return predictors from CRSP,
Compustat, I/B/E/S and other sources, publish the code (`github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection`) and
publish the resulting **portfolio returns**: for each predictor, the original paper's cut ("OP"),
plus deciles and quintiles, equal- and value-weighted, monthly and daily. The current release is
**2.0.0, October 2025** (signals translated from Stata to Python), with most returns to **December
2024**. Its signal documentation (`SignalDoc.csv`) records, per predictor, the original paper, its
key table, sample years, the weighting and quantile it used, and the return and t-statistic the
authors read from it - for `PS` (Piotroski): Table 3A, 1976-1996, value-weighted deciles, 1.958% a
month, t 5.594, with the note that the paper "does not explicitly say if it is equal or value
weighting, but we find more similar results using VW".

**How to get it.** The data page (`openassetpricing.com/data/`) links a public Google Drive folder.
No account, payment, sign-in or bot check is involved for the portfolio returns; the official
Python package `openassetpricing` (0.0.2, by Peng Li with the authors, GPLv2) and the R package
`OpenSourceAP.DownloadR` read the same folder. Only the stock-level signals that are pure CRSP
transformations (Price, Size, STreversal) need a WRDS subscription, which nothing here uses.

**Its terms.** The code repository is licensed **GPL-2.0** (read from the GitHub API and the
LICENSE file). Neither the data page nor the FAQ states a separate licence or restriction for the
data; the repository's README asks: *"If you use data or code based on our work, please cite the
paper."* No statement either permits or forbids republishing derived figures. That is the same
position as the French Data Library (a copyright line, no stated reuse terms), and the lab takes
the same course: it publishes the derived monthly series one experiment needs - here the two end
deciles' returns - with the citation and a link to the data page on the experiment and in the
page's footer, and never commits or republishes the files (they live in the gitignored
`data/lab/osap/`). If the owner wants more certainty than that, the authors can be asked; nothing
else on the site depends on this data.

**What happened when downloading (2026-10-20).** From 20:56 CT every file in the folder - and in
the 2024 release's folder - answered with Google's "Quota exceeded: too many users have viewed or
downloaded this file recently" page, which Google says can last up to 24 hours. The package

## 3. The paper, and where each figure came from

Joseph D. Piotroski, "Value investing: the use of historical financial statement information to
separate winners from losers", *Journal of Accounting Research* 38 (Supplement), 1-41 (2000),
doi:10.2307/2672906. Read with `pypdf` from the University of Chicago GSB "Selected Paper 84"
reprint (January 2002, 42 PDF pages; the link on the author's Wikipedia page,
`chicagobooth.edu/~/media/FE874EE65F624AAEBD0166B1974FD74D.pdf`). The reprint's printed page
numbers are used below; they are not the journal's.

- **Score** (pp. 6-9, Table 1 definitions p. 13): nine binary signals - ROA > 0, CFO > 0, ΔROA > 0,
  CFO > ROA (accruals), Δleverage < 0, Δliquidity > 0, no common equity issued, Δgross margin > 0,
  Δasset turnover > 0; F_SCORE is their sum, 0-9.
- **Sample** (pp. 10-11): each year 1976-1996, Compustat firms in the **highest book-to-market
  quintile** (cut points from the prior year's distribution) with the data to compute the signals:
  14,043 firm-years. Table 1's caption says "between 1975 and 1995"; the text says 1976-1996.
- **Returns** (p. 10): one-year buy-and-hold from the start of the fifth month after the fiscal year
  end; a delisted firm's delisting return is set to zero; market-adjusted = less the value-weighted
  market over the same window. Means are over firm-years, so equally weighted.
- **The table reproduced:** Table 3, Panel B (p. 17, one-year raw returns): Low F_SCORE (0-1, n 396)
  0.078; High F_SCORE (8-9, n 1,448) 0.313; High-Low 0.235, t 5.594. Panel A (p. 16,
  market-adjusted) gives 0.134, -0.096 and 0.230 (t 5.590); the abstract's "23% annual return" is
  that difference. Raw returns are used because the public portfolios are raw returns.
- **Not reproduced:** the size split (Table 4, p. 19: High-Low 0.270 small, 0.173 medium, **0.152
  among the largest third, t 1.884**); the share-price, volume and analyst-following splits
  (Table 5); the regressions (Table 6); the year-by-year record (18 of 21 years positive, p. 23-24).

What the paper does not say: whether the high-minus-low t-statistic accounts for overlap between
firm-years (it is a two-sample t-test, footnote f to Table 3); how firms with fiscal years ending in
different months are aligned in calendar time.

## 4. Tolerances, written and committed first

Commit `ce78f7d` holds the paper's figures and the tolerances. It was made while Google Drive was
still refusing every file, so **no OSAP return - not even a header - had been seen**. The record's
comment lists four construction differences known beforehand and the expectation they implied:
the direction reproduces, the spread lands below 23.5% and may be outside, the high end below
31.3%.

| Figure | Paper | Tolerance | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lowest-score decile, % a year | 7.8 | 4.2 | No standard error is reported for a group; it takes the spread's |
| Highest-score decile, % a year | 31.3 | 4.2 | The same |
| Highest minus lowest, % a year | 23.5 | 4.2 | One standard error of the paper's own spread (23.5 / 5.594 = 4.20) |
| Its t-statistic | 5.594 | 1.5 | Experiment 14's: one unit of t plus half for a different volatility |

**Plan rule 8**, checked from the record before committing: a result of no effect matches **0 of
4** (each end sits 11.75 points from the null's common level of 19.55; the spread and t miss).

**Comparison basis.** Equal weighting, because the paper's figures are means over firm-years.
Twelve times the average monthly return of OSAP's end deciles, January 1976 to December 1996 (252
months), against the paper's mean one-year raw return. OSAP re-forms its portfolios monthly on the
latest annual score, so a twelve-times-monthly average is not a year held; compounding alone would
put the paper's levels a point or two higher.

After the files arrived, one structural change was needed and made before the comparison was
built: the single-signal files are wide (`port01` ... `portLS` columns) where the full-set file is
long, so `read_osap` reads both. No figure or tolerance moved.

## 5. What reproduced and what did not

**1 of 4 within** (`lab.html#piotroski`).

| Figure | Paper | Here | Diff. | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lowest-score decile, % a year | 7.80 | 26.07 | +18.27 | outside |
| Highest-score decile, % a year | 31.30 | 30.41 | -0.89 | within |
| Highest minus lowest, % a year | 23.50 | 4.34 | -19.16 | outside |
| Its t-statistic | 5.594 | 1.52 | -4.08 | outside |

The miss is almost entirely at the low end, as the record predicted in direction though not in
size. The reason can be measured from OSAP's own files, which publish each portfolio's average
signal (`signallag`): over 1976-1996 the **lowest decile averaged a score of 2.69** (monthly range
1.66-3.71, about 140 firms) and the **highest 7.53** (7.23-8.16, about 148 firms). The paper's
groups are scores **0-1** (2.8% of its firm-years) and **8-9** (10.3%). With a score that takes
ten values the 10th and 90th percentile cut points tie, the end deciles absorb whole score values,
and the middle deciles are often empty (decile 2 has a return in one month of the 636). So the
public low group is mostly companies scoring 2-3, which the paper's own Table 3 Panel A puts close
to the high book-to-market average (score 2: -0.020; score 3: -0.015, market-adjusted), not at the
0-1 group's -0.096. The rerun checks a milder cut, and the milder cut separated far less.

**Weighted by market value - the authors' own cut - the gap is larger, not smaller:**

| Period | Equal-weighted spread, % a year (t) | Value-weighted spread, % a year (t) |
|---|---|---|
| The paper's years, 1976-1996 (252 months) | +4.34 (1.52) | **+12.58 (3.64)** |
| After the paper, 1997-2024 (336) | -2.00 (-0.55) | +7.49 (1.38) |
| Since 2007 (216) | -2.66 (-0.60) | +6.88 (1.03) |
| Whole record, 1972-2024 (636) | +0.79 (0.34) | +8.41 (2.51) |

That is the opposite of the paper's size pattern (Table 4, p. 19: strongest among small firms,
0.270; weakest among the largest third, 0.152, t 1.88). On the public construction, the small high
book-to-market companies that dominate an equal-weighted portfolio separated least. It is one
construction and one period, and the value-weighted series after 1997 cannot be told from zero.

**Volatility separated consistently.** In every period the lowest-score decile was the more
volatile: 7.36% against 5.27% a month in the paper's years equally weighted, 11.39% against 7.93%
after 1997 by market value.

## 6. What this does and does not verify

It verifies that a careful, documented, public construction of the Piotroski score - by authors
with no stake in the result, from CRSP and Compustat - earns over the paper's own years a
high-score end matching the paper's and a much smaller gap to its low-score end, because its end
groups are deciles rather than the paper's extreme scores. It also checks the published returns
are internally consistent: each month's long-short return equals decile 10 minus decile 1 in all
636 months, under both weightings.

It does **not** verify the paper's firm-level numbers (the lab sees no accounts); it does not test
the paper's 0-1 and 8-9 groups (OSAP publishes no such cut); it does not test among large
companies alone (no size split for this signal); and OSAP's score differs from the paper's in two
of nine signals (EBIT over sales for gross margin; a fall in shares outstanding for "no equity
issued").

## 7. What it implies for the screener's use of the score

No weight changes: the lab never sets a parameter, and this is one construction (rule 1; and
"How to read the evidence": a period under twenty years with t inside 2 is "not confirmed", not
"gone"). What it adds to the research question it bears on - `plan/factor-lab.md` question 5,
"What is Quality for?", and the `piotroski_conditional` rule:

1. **The paper's 23% is a statement about the extreme scores.** On deciles the gap is a fifth of
   that, equally weighted. The screener does not sort on extremes either: it ranks the score as a
   within-sector percentile, so a company's middle score counts as much as an extreme one. This
   rerun is evidence that the middle of the score carries much less return information than its
   ends - measured among high book-to-market companies only.
2. **Among large companies the evidence is not weaker than among small ones on this
   construction** - the value-weighted gap was the larger in every period. That is the part of the
   paper that matters most to a large-company screener, and the part its own Table 4 left weakest.
3. **Since publication neither weighting is distinguishable from zero** (t 1.38 and -0.55), like
   most results the lab has rerun.
4. **The low-score end is reliably the more volatile one.** That fits reading the score the way
   question 5 already frames Quality: a screen that keeps weak names out, rather than one that
   lifts strong ones.
5. **The rerun covers high book-to-market companies only, as the paper does.** The screener halves
   the score's weight for companies with a below-middle valuation score rather than removing it;
   nothing here measures the score among other companies, so it neither supports nor contradicts
   that half weight.

## 8. Second reading, 2026-10-23 (13:00 session)

`OWNER_FOCUS.md` 2026-10-20 item 2: a lab experiment is not checked until a reader who did not
build it has reopened the paper, re-read every reference figure, recomputed the measured ones and
confirmed in `git log` that the tolerances were committed before the comparison ran. For experiment
17 all three were done and **nothing needed correcting**. Two observations about its sources are
recorded below, because each is the kind of thing a later reader would otherwise read as an error.

**1. The four paper figures are exact.** Re-read in the Selected Paper 84 reprint
(`data/lab/papers/piotroski2000.pdf`, gitignored), **Table 3 Panel B, "One-Year Raw Returns",
printed page 17 (PDF page 19)**: Low F_SCORE mean **0.078**, High F_SCORE **0.313**, High-Low
**0.235**, t-statistic **5.594**. The firm-year counts the entry quotes are there too - 14,043 high
book-to-market firm-years, 396 in the low group (2.82%) and 1,448 in the high (10.31%) - and the
abstract's wording is as quoted: *"an investment strategy that buys expected winners and shorts
expected losers generates a 23% annual return between 1976 and 1996."* The highest **quintile** by
book-to-market is the paper's own cut (section 3.1), so the page's "highest fifth" is right, and
the nine signals it lists are the nine of section 2.3.4.

**2. Every measured figure reproduces under a second implementation.**

**Two observations on the sources, neither a defect here:**

- **OSAP's own metadata names the wrong panel.** `SignalDoc.csv` for `PS` gives
  `Key Table in OP = 3A` and `T-Stat = 5.594`. Those cannot both be right: 5.594 is **Panel B's**
  t-statistic, and Panel A - one-year *market-adjusted* returns - reports High-Low 0.230 with t
  **5.590**, and ends of -0.096 and 0.134 rather than 0.078 and 0.313. The experiment cites Panel B,
  which is where all four of its figures actually are, so it is right and more precise than the
  library it draws on. Recorded here so that a reader who compares the page with `SignalDoc.csv`
  does not conclude the panel was mis-cited. (`Return = 1.9583` in the same row is 23.5/12, Panel
  B's spread as a monthly figure, which points the same way.)
- **The `open_details` figure from Table 4 is market-adjusted.** "Among the largest third, high
  minus low 15.2%, t 1.88" is exact (Table 4, Large Firms, printed p. 19), but Table 4 reports
  one-year *market-adjusted* returns where the page's main comparison is raw. The right thing to
  compare it with is Panel A's all-sizes 23.0%, not Panel B's 23.5%, and on either basis the
  sentence's point - the effect is concentrated in small and medium companies - holds. The figure
  is not wrong and no number on the page changes; it is simply a basis the sentence does not name.

**Checked against the screener, not the paper:** the `in_the_screener` sentence says the score is
16% of Quality and Quality 22% of the composite, "so about 3.5 points", halved where the valuation
score is below the middle. `config.yaml` has `piotroski_f_score: 16` in the Quality table,
`quality: 22`, and `piotroski_conditional.reduction_factor: 0.5` - 0.16 x 22 = 3.52. Correct as
printed. (The bank profile scores it 15; the sentence does not claim otherwise.)
